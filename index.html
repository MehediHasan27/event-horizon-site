Strong-field optics — No. 14 Drag the hole

Ray-traced null geodesics

A disk seen over the top of itself

Turn an accretion disk edge-on and gravity lifts its far side into an arch above the shadow, and drops a second copy below. One flat ring, three images, no perspective trick involved.

Shadow √27 ⁄ 2 ≈ 2.598 rs
Photon sphere 1.5 rs
Orbit Ω ∝ r−3⁄2
Integrator 112 steps, adaptive

A thin accretion disk is flat. Tilt it almost edge-on to the camera and you should see a line — a bright bar crossing in front of the hole, with the far half hidden behind it. That is what a camera in flat space would record.

What a camera near a black hole records instead is an arch. Light leaving the top of the disk's far side is bent downward toward you and arrives from above the shadow; light leaving the underside is bent upward and arrives from below. The flat ring is wrapped over and under itself, so the disk appears to pass behind the hole and then over it, closing into a halo. Both arcs are the same material, imaged twice.

Marching photons, not pixels

You cannot get that arch from a radial screen-space distortion. Wrapping the far side over the top is a genuinely three-dimensional effect: it depends on where each photon's path crosses the disk plane, and some paths cross it more than once. So the background here is integrated rather than warped. For every pixel a ray is fired from the camera and stepped through curved space under

a = −1.5 · h² · r⃗ ⁄ r⁵

the Schwarzschild geodesic acceleration, with h the photon's angular momentum, conserved and so computed once at launch. Each step tests whether the ray has crossed the disk plane, and if the crossing radius lies inside the disk, that patch's emission is added. Rays that fall inside rs return black. Rays that escape sample the starfield along their final, deflected direction — which is why the star pattern near the rim is stretched and, in places, doubled.

Because the crossing test runs at every step, a single ray can pick up the disk two or three times: once in front, once arching over, once beneath. The halo is not drawn. It falls out of the integration.

The material itself is not spinning as a rigid plate. Orbital speed falls off as r−3⁄2, so the inner edge laps the outer many times over and anything drawn on the disk is pulled into filaments — long streaks along the flow, fine detail across it. That shear is the whole texture. The bright wisps are advected at the local orbital rate and smeared by it, which is why the pattern reorganises continuously instead of coming back round.

The hole drifts, too. Nothing anchors a black hole to the middle of a page; this one wanders on two slow incommensurate sines per axis, so the path takes minutes rather than seconds and does not audibly repeat. Grab it and put it somewhere — the wander resumes from wherever you let go.

Where the type goes

The text is lensed too, but by a cheaper map — the analytic point-mass lens, applied per glyph. Each character's resting position is treated as a source at radius β and drawn at the radius its primary image would occupy, θ = ½(β + √(β² + 4θE²)). Far out, type sits exactly where it was set. Close in, every glyph is pushed onto the Einstein radius, so words crossing behind the hole pile onto a ring rather than disappearing into it.

Each glyph is also sampled a fraction of an em to either side; the offset between where those two samples land gives the local tangent and the local stretch. That is what leans the letters into the curve instead of stepping them around it upright.

Two different models, then: an integrator for the sky and a closed-form lens for the words. They share a centre and a scale, which is enough to read as one field. Doing the type properly would mean tracing a geodesic per glyph per frame, and the visible difference would not pay for it.

What is accurate and what is not

The geometry is real. The shadow's apparent radius is √27⁄2 ≈ 2.6 Schwarzschild radii rather than 1, because light grazing the photon sphere is captured; the arch, the doubled stars and the thin bright ring hugging the shadow all come out of the integration rather than being painted in.

The disk's own brightness is invented — banded noise, hot white at the inner edge falling to amber outward. And it is deliberately near-symmetric. A real disk this close is strongly Doppler-boosted, with the approaching side far brighter than the receding one; the version audiences know had that asymmetry dialled out, on the grounds that a lopsided disk read as a mistake rather than as physics. This render follows that choice, which makes it a portrait rather than a measurement.
